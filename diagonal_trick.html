The Diagonal Trick

A cheaper way to say which way something points, with no sine, cosine or arctangent anywhere in it.

First, the same thing in plain trigonometry

If you already know tan, you can meet the trick there. Take any angle and make a number from its sine and cosine, but with sizes added instead of squares:

d = sin / ( |sin| + |cos| ) always between −1 and 1, and it still grows with the angle

That number is the dangent. It is a stand-in for the angle, and the tangent drops straight out of it with one division:

tan = d / ( 1 − |d| ) exact, for angles between −90° and 90° y / x = sign(x) · sign(d) · |d| / ( 1 − |d| ) going backwards from a point (x, y): d = y / (|x| + |y|), keep the sign of x

Nothing here is an approximation. The only price is that d is not the angle itself. Try it:

The sine and cosine in d are only there to explain it. In practice you never compute them: you have (x, y) already, and d = y / (|x| + |y|) needs neither. The pieces below show what that buys. If you want to play with the full system, including the fast polynomial form, there is a Desmos graph.

Aim at the Diagonal instead of measuring an angle

Edges, lines and arrows all have a direction. The usual way to write a direction down is an angle in degrees, and finding that angle from a pair of numbers (x, y) takes atan2, which is slow and awkward on small chips.

Here is the shortcut. Draw a square standing on its corner, a Diagonal, around the centre. A direction is just a ray from the middle, and that ray hits the Diagonal at one spot. Walk around the Diagonal from the right-hand corner, count the corners as 0, 1, 2, 3, and the number where you stop is the direction. Drag the dot or use the slider and watch both measures.

The recipe is three steps: add up the sizes of x and y, divide y by that total, and add which quarter you are in. That is the whole thing. One division, a few additions, and no trigonometry. The two numbers on the bottom lines of the readout are the same direction written two ways.

The steps are not the same size

Equal walking distances on the Diagonal are not equal turns of the ray. Slice the Diagonal into equal pieces and the wedges that result are narrower where the ray points along an axis and wider on the diagonals. The widest is up to twice the narrowest, and the ratio climbs toward 2 to 1 as the slices get finer.

For many jobs that does not matter, because the number is only used to put directions in order or into buckets. If you do need real degrees, you convert once at the end, or look the answer up in a small table.

What you can do without leaving the Diagonal

Put directions in order

A bigger Diagonal position always means a bigger turn, so sorting by it gives the same order as sorting by angle.

sort(by: position)

Drop directions into buckets

Multiply the position by the number of buckets you want and drop the fraction. Edge pixels can then vote for the direction they face.

bucket = floor(pos × n / 4)

Get the slope back

Inside the first quarter the position f turns back into the slope y/x with one more division. The other quarters use the flipped form.

slope = f / (1 − f)

Where it earns its keep

A lane-keeping line finder asks every edge pixel which way it faces, then lets that pixel vote for lines running that way. Millions of pixels means millions of direction questions, so the cost of each one matters. The finder built on this idea keeps its direction as a Diagonal position, splits it into 128 buckets, and never calls atan2, sin, cos or a square root per pixel.

≈ 2×faster than the same vote with atan2 buckets, at the same accuracy
3.9–5.0×faster than a textbook integer Hough sweep in the author's sandbox build
128direction buckets, each about 0.9° to 1.8° wide over a half turn

Timings are single-thread scalar C on straight-edge test scenes and one dashcam frame. Curved, thin and blurred features have not been tested.

What it costs

  • The buckets are uneven by up to 2 to 1, as the second picture shows. A job that needs perfectly equal angle steps should convert first.
  • Averaging two directions, or turning by a set number of degrees, does not work on Diagonal positions directly. Convert, do the work, convert back.

The idea is old. The graphics book GPU Gems uses a pseudo-angle to order the corners of a slicing polygon, citing Moret and Shapiro (1991): chapter 39, Algorithm 39-2. In this project the Diagonal position is called the dangent.
